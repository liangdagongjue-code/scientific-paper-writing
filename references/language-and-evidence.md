# Language, Evidence, and Revision Guide

This guide governs expression and verification across subjects. It does not supply scientific facts for a new manuscript.

## 1. Paragraph construction

Give each paragraph one principal job. A useful default is a topic or question, the reasoning or evidence needed to address it, and a consequence that advances the argument. Do not append a summary sentence when it merely repeats the opening in new words.

Move from known context to new information. Reuse a precise technical term when continuity matters; swapping arbitrary synonyms can accidentally change the concept. Introduce distinctions when they become necessary, rather than front-loading all terminology.

Cut repeated functions, empty evaluative phrases, and irrelevant process explanations. Preserve meaningful qualifications and causal links. “Shorter” is not a sufficient reason to delete the sentence that explains why the next paragraph follows.

Avoid manuscript-as-audit-log prose: file-generation details, drafting history, citation-checking procedures, and editorial instructions are normally not scientific findings. Place legitimate acknowledgments, methods disclosures, ethics information, and required declarations in their appropriate locations rather than erasing them as stylistic clutter.

## 2. Transitions and word choice

Choose a connector only after identifying the relationship:

| Relationship | Possible expression | Required logical condition |
|---|---|---|
| Tension or contrast | however; whereas; in contrast | The two claims genuinely differ in a relevant way |
| Consequence | therefore; thus; consequently | The preceding reasoning supports the inference |
| Narrower focus | specifically; more directly | The discussion narrows rather than switches topics |
| Related but distinct approach | a complementary line of work | Its connection to the present question is explained |
| Mechanism to capability | by … thereby …; this enables … | The stated capability follows from the described operation |
| Conditional scope | when; under; provided that | The condition matters to the claim's validity |
| Insight to proposal | building on this insight | An actual insight has already been explained |

Do not decorate a disconnected argument with repeated “Furthermore” and “Moreover.” Sometimes conceptual continuity makes a connector unnecessary.

Prefer concrete verbs such as estimate, identify, reconstruct, compare, characterize, establish, explain, or preserve when they describe the actual work. Replace “a novel, powerful, effective framework” with the particular capability and its basis. Avoid exaggerated adverbs and repeated promotional labels.

Use assertive wording for established contributions and calibrated wording for interpretation. “May,” “can,” and “suggests” are not prohibited. A proved or measured fact should not be diluted by habitual “might”; a tentative mechanism should not be promoted to a demonstrated causal law.

Distinguish:

- **substantial or marked:** a claim about magnitude or practical importance, requiring suitable evidence;
- **statistically significant:** a claim about an appropriate analysis and threshold, not merely a large-looking difference;
- **consistent across evaluated settings:** a scoped statement that must match all settings it describes;
- **universal, first, optimal, state of the art:** strong claims requiring unusually specific support and, for novelty/comparison claims, adequate literature and evaluation coverage.

The goal is confident precision, not either defensive hedging or marketing.

## 3. Evidence and citation ownership

For each substantial sentence, distinguish its source: prior literature, our observation, an assumption, a derivation, an interpretation, or a future proposal. Express that status in the sentence when ambiguity would matter.

Cite primary work next to a borrowed method, external empirical fact, or nontrivial premise. Verify that the source supports the exact wording. Do not cite an article merely because it uses similar terminology. Check the actual publication/version metadata instead of inventing venue acceptance or a DOI.

Original method descriptions and results do not require unrelated citations attached to each sentence. They require our own explanations, analysis, and evidence. Borrowed components, datasets, software, instruments, and conceptual frameworks still need proper attribution.

Prefer verified published scholarship where appropriate. Keep foundational sources if they explain the idea's origin, and use newer work when it materially advances the relevant topic. Unpublished work can affect novelty even when absent from the manuscript bibliography. If it must be discussed, represent its status accurately and follow applicable author/venue constraints. Do not turn a previous project's citation preference into a universal ban.

For a new scientific claim, retrieve and inspect adequate source material when required. If verification is unavailable, state the uncertainty in the working draft or ask for the source; do not invent a plausible citation. Learn organizational techniques from model papers without copying distinctive phrasing or rearranging near-verbatim sentences.

Quantitative claims must retain the right numerator, denominator, unit, comparator, uncertainty, and aggregation. Percentage-point change and relative percentage change are different. Sample count and independent replication count may differ. Qualitative or historical claims also require traceable provenance and context.

Do not infer causal attribution from association, statistical significance from an effect size, external validity from one setting, or implementation equivalence from a shared method name. Stronger phrasing is an improvement only if the evidence supports it.

## 4. Translation and approved drafts

The author's default for substantial new text is Chinese conceptual review followed by formal English. Direct requests for English override that default. Do not repeatedly ask for approval when the current request already authorizes drafting or integration.

For an approved draft, translate its complete reasoning, not only its main points. Preserve equations, numbers, conditions, comparator definitions, citation ownership, and claim strength. Natural academic English can change sentence structure and remove language-specific redundancy without changing substantive coverage.

If a scientific inconsistency becomes apparent, identify it rather than silently smoothing it into a stronger statement. Resolve substantive corrections explicitly. If a prior revision was rejected, do not reintroduce the rejected compression under the label of translation or polishing.

For a literal translation of another paper, preserve its structure and claims. Applying the author's preferred writing style to an unrequested rewrite would be a different task.

## 5. Revision and delivery

Review from largest to smallest: central question and answer → section roles → paragraph progression → evidence and terminology → sentence economy → grammar and formatting. Polishing individual sentences cannot repair a missing gap or an unsupported contribution.

For major drafting, an internal claim–evidence map can prevent gaps: state each main claim, its evidence source, applicable conditions, and planned location. Do not include this working map in the paper unless it serves a scientific purpose.

Adapt a checklist to the requested edit rather than restarting the entire research process:

1. Does the abstract state the contribution and meaningful finding without becoming a protocol inventory?
2. Does the introduction make the research question necessary and situate prior capabilities fairly?
3. Does the literature discussion explain relationships rather than list citations or repeatedly promote the current method?
4. Are definitions and assumptions sufficient, and does the approach follow from the problem?
5. Does each result answer a stated question with the correct metric, scope, and evidence?
6. Are figures, tables, and text complementary instead of redundant or contradictory?
7. Does the conclusion state what was established without introducing new unsupported claims?
8. Are citations, terminology, supplementary material, and required declarations consistent?

When file integration is requested, work on the correct current files and maintain relevant templates and cross-references within scope. Verify the actual delivered artifact; a source edit is not a successful PDF build. Do not declare submission, acceptance, or global installation of a skill without performing and verifying that separate action.

Research evidence and current policies govern relevant disclosure, ethics, and reporting obligations. A previous author's request to remove a paragraph in one draft is not a reusable exemption. Conversely, do not insert procedural commentary into scientific sections merely because it appeared in an internal record.

## 6. Cross-paper transfer test

Before reusing guidance, separate three levels:

| Level | Transferable? | Examples |
|---|---|---|
| Reasoning principle | Yes | Establish a gap, explain mechanisms, match claims to evidence, preserve logical bridges |
| Author preference | Default, adaptable | Chinese-first review, concise abstracts, literature-first related work, compact conclusion |
| Project decision or fact | No, unless relevant and explicitly adopted | Algorithm names, exact task counts, metrics, color codes, omitted sections, figure numbers, venue-specific format |

Test the outline mentally against a different genre. A theory paper should not acquire invented experiments; a structured clinical abstract should not be forced into one paragraph; a review should not acquire a new-method contribution it does not make. An efficiency paper must not omit the measurements needed for its central efficiency claim. A short empirical paper need not acquire a standalone section for every function.

Transfer the reasoning habits. Rebuild the scientific content from the new study.
